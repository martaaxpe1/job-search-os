## Compensation

### "What is your current pay?"
**Strategy:** Don't share. You're freelancing — an hourly rate doesn't translate to a permanent salary comparison and will anchor the conversation wrong.
**Script:** "I'm currently freelancing, so comparing day rates to a permanent package isn't straightforward. What matters to me is finding the right role — could you share the budgeted range for this position?"
**If pressed hard:** "I'm targeting €100–110K gross for permanent roles at this level, based on market research for innovation leadership roles in the Netherlands."
**Note:** Never share the €140/h freelance rate — it reads as €280K+ annualised and will price you out or create awkward expectation gaps.

### "What are your compensation expectations?"
**Target range:** €100,000–€110,000 gross/year (permanent, 40h/week)
**Floor:** €80,000 gross/year — below this is a dealbreaker
**Script:** "Based on my research for innovation leadership roles in the Netherlands and my 12 years of experience — including team management, budget ownership, and full product launches — I'm targeting €100–110K gross. I'm open to discussing the full package including pension, bonus, and development budget."
**If the role is clearly IC (not leadership):** Adjust to "€85–100K" and lead with the IC framing.
**Freelance rates (if asked):** Management roles: €140/h. IC/research roles: €110/h. Day rate = hourly × 8.

## Common Questions

### "Why are you leaving your current role?" / "Why are you looking?"
**Context:** You're a freelancer, not leaving a permanent role. This is actually a strength — you chose to go independent, took on meaningful projects, and are now deliberately choosing to go back in-house.
**Script:** "After my role at Culligan, I chose to go freelance to take on a few specific projects — I worked with YouTube on their CS service design, and with a small AI startup on their product strategy. It's been a valuable chapter, but I'm ready to embed properly in a team again. I want to build something for the long term — own a programme, grow a team, see initiatives through to scale. Freelancing gives you breadth; in-house gives you depth. I'm ready for the depth."
**Variation for consulting/agency roles:** "I've been freelancing since mid-2025, and I'm genuinely open to both permanent and contract engagements. For the right role, I'd happily embed full-time."

### "Why do you want to work at [company]?"
**Note:** This is customised per company during `/interview-prep`. Pre-loaded angles by company type:
- **IKEA/INGKA:** "IKEA sits at the intersection of physical and digital experience at a scale almost no other company matches. And the sustainability mandate is real, not cosmetic — the way you've approached circular design and material innovation is exactly the kind of long-game thinking I want to be part of. I've spent the last few years building innovation programmes inside complex organisations, and IKEA's scale of impact is what gets me out of bed."
- **Water/sustainability companies (Quooker, Marie-Stella-Maris, Ocean Cleanup):** Lead with the personal water sustainability commitment — Pellican/Culligan origin story, Delft festival research, genuine belief that clean water access is an underrated lever for quality of life. Make it specific to the company's own mission.
- **Large corporates (Philips, Unilever, DSM-Firmenich):** Lead with scale + craft — "I've seen what it looks like to build innovation programmes in complex organisations. The best work happens when you have the resources to actually run proper experiments, not just slide decks. That's what [company] offers."
- **Agencies/consulting (Accenture Song, frog, Designit):** "I went in-house to get the client-side perspective — to understand what it actually takes to move a validated concept into production, past all the internal friction. Coming back to consulting now, I bring that lens. I know what the client is really asking for even when they can't articulate it."

### "What is your greatest weakness?"
**Chosen weakness:** Breadth over depth — being an industry generalist in a world that often rewards domain specialists.
**Script:** "My natural instinct is to go wide before going deep — I've worked across water, FMCG, fintech, big tech, aviation. That breadth has given me a kind of cross-industry pattern recognition I genuinely value: I can see when a solution that worked in fintech could apply to consumer goods, or vice versa. But I've learned that in rooms full of domain specialists, you have to earn credibility differently. I've gotten better at leading with the underlying competency — the discovery method, the experimentation rigour, the team dynamic — before the domain context. And I've learned to be genuinely curious about the company's domain rather than just neutral about it. The water and sustainability space is where I actually have that passion — which is why I'm deliberately targeting companies in that space."
**Why this works:** It's real, it connects to the Adidas lesson (without naming Adidas), and it shows self-awareness and a mitigation strategy.

### "What is your biggest failure?"
**Chosen story:** The payment pilot in Italy — a pilot that failed partly because of poor design decisions I owned.
**Script:** "One of the clearest failures I was responsible for was a payment pilot we ran in Italy at Culligan. We were testing whether we could turn office water dispensers into pay-per-use machines — replacing single-use plastic bottles in SMEs. The commercial model required a wallet-based payment system, which was technically more complex than a standard POS but the only viable path on margin. So far, so reasonable.

The failure was in how we ran the pilot. The site was too small, the user base wasn't tech-savvy enough to give us a clean signal on the payment UX, and I made the mistake of letting marketing run the execution rather than keeping the innovation team in control. By the time results came in, almost nobody had used it, and leadership called it a failure.

My read was different — we had learned the business model math could work at volume, the retrofit was technically feasible, and the user profile matters enormously for this kind of experience. But because the pilot conditions were poor, those learnings got buried under the outcome.

What changed after that: I introduced tighter site selection criteria, required success metrics to be agreed upfront before any pilot launched, and the innovation team kept control of execution rather than handing off to a function with different incentives. The pilot failed. The learning didn't have to."
**Why this works:** Real failure with real stakes. Shows intellectual honesty (doesn't hide behind "the conditions were bad" — she owns the design decisions). Shows systems thinking (the fix was structural, not just "try harder"). Turns a failure into evidence of leadership maturity.

### "Where do you see yourself in 3 years?"
**Script:** "In three years, I want to be leading an innovation team that is genuinely changing how a company thinks about what it builds next. Not just running workshops or producing slide decks — actually owning a pipeline of validated bets, with a team of researchers and designers I've helped grow. I want the work to be about things that matter — water, sustainability, experiences people actually use every day. And I want the company's name on my CV to mean something — to be the kind of place where serious innovation people want to work. I'm ambitious about the work, but I'm patient about the path. The best innovation outcomes I've seen took three to five years to materialise from first insight to market. I want to be in a place that gives me that runway."

### "Tell me about yourself" (2 min max)
**Framework:** Origin → consulting foundation → in-house pivot → what changed → what's next → company hook
**Script:**
"I'm originally from Spain — I studied product design in Barcelona, then came to the Netherlands for my Master's in Strategic Product Design at TU Delft. That set the direction: design as a way into business problems, not just product problems.

From there I went into consulting — spent several years at a firm called INSITUM, doing ethnographic and design research across FMCG, big tech, and healthcare in Latin America and Europe. That's where I got serious about the early stages of innovation and applied design thinking in real business contexts.

After that I led service design and UX research at an agency in Rotterdam, building that practice from scratch. And then I went in-house. I spent almost five years at Culligan as Global Head of Innovation — running discovery, experimentation, and pilots across physical, digital, and service layers. The water category is interesting territory: sustainability, consumer behaviour, physical-digital product intersections. We built an innovation pipeline, and one of the initiatives I led grew into a new flavoured water category that launched in both the US and EU markets.

That in-house experience changed how I think about innovation work. I've been on the client side — navigating corporate inertia, managing cross-functional teams, building business cases for leadership. I understand what it actually takes to move something from a validated concept to a live proposition. That gap between 'great idea' and 'this is actually in people's hands' is where most innovation work breaks down, and it's where I've spent the most time.

Right now I'm freelancing — doing a service design project for YouTube — but I'm looking for the right permanent role to embed somewhere for the long term. Which is what drew me to [company] — [customise per role]."

**Timing note:** Practise to 90 seconds. The last line is an invitation — make the company hook specific enough that they want to pick it up.

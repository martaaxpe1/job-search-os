### Story 2: YouTube — End-to-End Service Design (Front + Backstage)
- **Situation:** YouTube's customer support experience had structural pain points across channels — for both end-users and the CS agents supporting them (outsourced). The product design team needed to understand the full journey, front-end and back-end, to prioritise where Salesforce (SF) redesign could reduce friction at scale.
- **Task:** Lead qualitative discovery for both consumers and agents — map the complete support journey, surface structural pain points, and hand off a prioritised opportunity backlog that would directly feed SF workflow prototyping and development.
- **Action:** Ran a multi-method discovery: diary studies (first touchpoint — participants documented real support interactions, capturing contextual behaviour and emotional states); 2-hour semi-structured immersions (deep-diving into experiences with activities); journey mapping (synthesising into a detailed map across channels, user types, from product usage through future CS vision); and 2-day stakeholder share-outs with workshops to align on key problems, opportunities, and a prioritised backlog. Used an empathy-led methodology with metaphor elicitation to get to the deeper "why." Designed SF workflows with internal teams and prototyped new agent flows, testing with Tier 1 agents, SMEs, and team leads before handoff to development. Navigated a complex stakeholder group: vendor managers, product service managers, SF implementation team, business leads, and outsourced agents.
- **Result:** Delivered a prioritised opportunity backlog, SF workflow prototypes tested and iterated with agents, and three key structural insights: (1) consumers are divided on AI for support — trust AI for low-stakes, want human for sensitive/urgent issues; (2) agents operated with massively fragmented tooling and outdated information, and performance metrics were inadvertently driving workarounds and call deflection; (3) self-service is users' first instinct, but current help-center experiences are maze-like — people use AI companions or Reddit instead.
- **Tags:** service-design, research, backstage, CS-operations, AI-for-support, multi-method, big-tech, complex-stakeholders

### Story 5: The Culligan Innovation Pipeline (System-Building)
- **Situation:** Culligan had no structured approach to early-stage innovation — no pipeline, no experimentation framework, no user research discipline at the global level. Innovation was isolated and had limited visibility with leadership. The team had a deliberately ambidextrous mandate: independent exploration of radical ideas (which business units couldn't or wouldn't pursue themselves) alongside close collaboration with operating businesses on incremental capability-building work. Holding both simultaneously without letting either collapse into the other was the core challenge.
- **Task:** As Global Head of Innovation, design and run a repeatable innovation system across a global company — owning the roadmap, the budget, the team, and all stakeholder relationships across leadership, BUs, finance, and external partners.
- **Action:** Designed the stage-gate funnel from scratch — deliberately simple, three stages. Stage 1: time-boxed 8-week POC with independent exploration, clear hypotheses, and defined metrics. BU involvement intentionally excluded at this stage (too early — BU pull collapses work toward incremental before you've found out if something genuinely new is possible). Gate: presentation to leadership and relevant BUs. Stage 2: proving fit — does the solution work inside a specific BU, and does the business model hold up? BU collaboration became essential here; gate decision became a shared call. Stage 3: scaling and handover — BU owns decisions, innovation team acts as enabler, not driver. Accountability transferred intentionally. Scouting was continuous: tracking operating business challenges, pulling consumer insights from marketing, tracking external trends, and working with the M&A team on make-or-buy decisions. Owned the full budget — approximately €450K OpEx and CapEx combined annually — including finance relationship management, capital appropriation business cases for individual initiatives, and defining the following year's budget. Communicated progress to leadership frequently, using touchpoints to validate direction against business strategy, not just report status. The COO was the early sponsor, which gave the team genuine air cover during uncertain exploratory phases.
- **Result:** Innovation pipeline of 16+ POCs and 6 pilots established. Pelican ($2.5M projected revenue + Culligan's IoT infrastructure) was the flagship output. [NEEDS METRIC: other pipeline outcomes — how many progressed to scale?]

### Story 11: Killing Your Darlings — The Smart Bottle
- **Situation:** Alongside the Pelican connected dispenser work, the team developed a concept for a reusable smart bottle that could interact with Culligan's dispensers — personalising the experience, tracking consumption, creating a feedback loop between user and machine. A prototype was built and a vision video produced to show the interaction model across different levels of maturity.
- **Task:** Make a hard prioritisation call between two innovation bets with genuinely different time horizons — one with a clear near-term commercial path (flavoured water), one with a compelling long-term vision but no near-term fundability (smart bottle).
- **Action:** Assessed both against available resources and organisational readiness. Flavoured water had active BU interest, a clearer commercial path, and was fundable in the near term. The smart bottle was further out, harder to quantify, and required the business to commit to a future it couldn't fully picture yet. Made the call to pause the smart bottle and concentrate resources on Pelican. Crucially: didn't let the thinking disappear. The prototype and vision video became a reference point — a rough roadmap for the future of the dispenser experience — even with the active project paused. Created an artifact the business could pick up later, rather than losing the work entirely.
- **Result:** Pelican progressed to market. The smart bottle artefacts remain a live reference for the dispenser experience roadmap. The project wasn't killed because it was a bad idea — it was paused because the organisation couldn't hold two horizons simultaneously under resource pressure. That's a different thing, and the distinction mattered for team morale and for keeping the door open.
- **Lesson:** In a resource-constrained innovation team, the discipline is knowing which ideas are ahead of their time versus genuinely wrong — and finding a way to keep the ahead-of-their-time ones alive in some form, even if just as an artefact someone picks up later.

### Story 12: Brand as Blocker — Navigating a Constraint That Wouldn't Move
- **Situation:** At Culligan, the marketing team was protective of the brand — legitimately so. But that protection was applied uniformly, including to small-scale pilots with 50 users that had zero public visibility. The practical consequence: the innovation team couldn't run the fast, cheap digital experiments most lean teams rely on. No landing pages, no social ads to test demand signals, no online concept testing under the brand. Every time we wanted to use the brand for any early-stage experiment, we hit a wall.
- **Task:** Keep the innovation pipeline moving in an environment where one of the standard experimentation tools — brand-attributed digital testing — was effectively unavailable.
- **Action:** Accepted the constraint as one that wouldn't move (marketing's concern was legitimate even if the scale didn't warrant it, and there was no authority to override them). Adapted the approach: leaned much harder into qualitative and prototype-based methods, which are slower but give richer signal. In some cases tested concepts without brand attribution at all, which actually produced cleaner data on the proposition itself, free of brand halo effects. Didn't fight every blocker — focused energy on designing around the ones that wouldn't move.
- **Result:** The pipeline kept moving. The qualitative lean gave richer signal than digital tests would have at that stage. The brand relationship stayed intact.
- **What I'd do differently:** Create a dedicated experimentation brand — deliberately fictional, built for early-stage pilots — and co-design it with marketing upfront so results are accepted as valid proxies. One investment, reusable infrastructure for experimentation. The key is getting marketing's explicit agreement during the design phase, not after results are in.
- **Tags:** stakeholder-management, constraint-navigation, experimentation, corporate-innovation, influence-without-authority
- **Best for:** "Tell me about a time you faced a stakeholder who blocked your work," "How do you operate in a corporate environment with lots of constraints," "What would you do differently," "Tell me about a time you had to adapt your approach"

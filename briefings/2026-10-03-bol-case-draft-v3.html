Working title — alternatives: "Designers as makers" · "The bol designer in 2028" · "Less delivery, more discovery".

Storyline in one breath: AI is changing what a designer's day is made of. My vision is a bol designer who works as a maker and spends most of their time on discovery. That only works if people change how they behave, so the standard I'd introduce is a behaviour framework, made practical through one way of working. I'd land it in six months by starting with one squad and the people most sceptical of it, measure whether behaviour actually changes, and start by making the Heads of Design's job easier.

bolMARTA AXPE

Strategic goal

By 2028, every designer at bol works as a maker: creating artefacts that open a dialogue with every craft, and spending more time discovering what's worth building for the future of e-commerce.

What a maker does

Turns an idea into something working, on real data and in code, in days. Not a deck or a static screen: something people can react to, question and improve.

Why discovery

When AI takes over production, the value moves to deciding what to build. Time spent testing ideas is how bol shapes what shopping becomes, for customers and partners.

Never alone

Makers work with product, engineering and data, not around them. The artefact is how the crafts think together, not a handoff between them.

This is the vision for the team. A maker isn't a designer working alone in code: it's a designer whose artefacts start the right conversations with every other craft, so bol spends its time on what's worth building next.

In an AI-first craft, the standard is how people work

Tools will change every quarter. Behaviours are what make any tool produce good work. So the first standard I'd introduce isn't a pixel spec: it's four behaviours, co-written with designers, researchers and writers, then made practical through one way of working.

Universal vs local: the behaviours are the same everywhere. How they show up is local: a partner-tools designer and a consumer designer will use different evidence and different data.

07

Standards and frameworks in this era are about how people behave. These four are the ones I'd start from. The specific wording gets co-written with the people who'll live by it; that's the "build it with your users" part of the brief.

2 · Standards & frameworks

One loop turns the behaviours into daily work

Example: one designer's itch, through the loop Hypothesis

"I spend two days making variations for a review, and the decision still comes down to opinion."

FrameWhat does the review need to decide, and what would prove it?
ExploreAI lists edge cases and gaps in the options, instead of producing more variations.
MakeOne working prototype on real data, in a day.
DecideThe review discusses the prototype and the evidence. The decision and its reason are written down.
EncodeWhat we learned becomes intent in the design language, so the next draft starts closer.
ObserveAfter release, live behaviour shows whether it holds.

The standard behind it, "show it working": every proposal arrives as a working artefact, with its hypothesis and the real data it was tested on.

Experience exampleI replaced research reports with design sprints; developers started asking for the format at kick-off.

I've done this without authority before: Culligan

A design language from zero, adopted by teams that didn't report to me

I owned the UX layer: who the users are, their journeys, and one UX character (simple, trustworthy, expert) adapted to each context. I co-designed the touchscreen, indicator and accessibility toolkits with our agency, and approved the work.

One colour, one meaning, on plastic and pixels
  • Azure marks critical touch points on the hardware and the filtration process on screen
  • Indicators: white all well, yellow attention, orange act now
Motion that tells the truth about urgency
  • Calm for steady information; pulsing only when attention is critical
  • A breathing light, so the product feels at work
Rules from real use
  • 1–2 options: selecting is dispensing. 3 or more: separate steps
  • Shared office: reset to the most-used option. Home: remember the last choice
Insert: layer-4 visuals — indicator examples, BFC screens across tiers

Short term

One six-layer language. Each category redesigned its flagship on it.

Long term

It outlived my involvement. The BFC, the first product with all six layers, won a Red Dot.

What I'd change

Approval ended up in one central brand gate. At hardware speed that works; at AI speed it's the bottleneck.

How it maps to bol: craft first, then a shared language, then assets that make teams independent, then support. And this time, distributed ownership through a designer council rather than one gate.

Risks and known unknowns
  • Cognitive load and burnout
  • Journey ownership clashing with domain accountability
  • The cost of keeping a design language usable by agents
  • Compute and energy cost
  • People changing code they don't understand
  • Simulated evidence mistaken for real research
When energy dips
  • Measure contribution, not attendance
  • If a ritual isn't earning its time after two cycles, shrink it or stop it
  • Rotate hosts; show work, not slides
  • Go to the sceptics first and ask why
Quality as a loop, decided by reversibility

Fix fast where an experience is reversible and low-risk. Prevent upfront where the stakes are high: checkout, payments, partner payouts, trust.

Context is the infrastructure (SunSpot)

I built findsunspot.com with AI tools. The interface looked polished, but shade was calculated from building centre points, not terrace shapes. I fixed it by feeding in real data, the city's terrace permit polygons. Generic output is a missing-input problem, not a prompting problem.

## 2. The scale question

Thijmen flagged this one: how you'd handle the scale of bol. Name the problem Maurice and Matthew described first, then give your answer.

### The answer (about 85 seconds)

> From what Maurice and Matthew told me, engineering is already further ahead with AI, and teams are waiting for design to deliver. At bol's scale that adds up: the whole organisation ends up moving at design's pace. So design has to catch up, and not by me reviewing more work. That would just add another queue.
>
> I'd run the design workflow as a product, with designers as its users, and build it together with engineering, product and analytics. Most of the lost time sits in the hand-offs between crafts, so no craft can fix it alone. Design owns the intent. Engineering owns the infrastructure it runs on.
>
> And I wouldn't start with a big programme. I'd find the biggest burden that's also the quickest win, fix it with the other crafts, and get the engine running. Then the next one. That's what gives you agility between the crafts, not just inside each one.
>
> It's how IoT grew at Culligan, the global leader in drinking water. It started as a few self-funded pilots. Once they proved their value, the business units put IoT in their own roadmaps, and today 80% of Culligan's commercial products run on it.

**Then hand it back:** *"Is that how it looks from the engineering side? Where do you see teams waiting on design most?"* He knows that side better than anyone else in this process, and his answer tells you which follow-up to use. If you ask it here, skip question 4 in section 4.

**30-second version:**
> Engineering is ahead with AI and teams are waiting for design, so design has to catch up first. I'd run the design workflow as a product, built with the other crafts, and start with the biggest burden that's also the quickest win, to get things moving fast. That's how IoT grew at Culligan: a few self-funded pilots, and now 80% of their commercial products run on it.

### Follow-ups

1. **"What would the first quick win be?"**
   - *"I'd want to see how the work flows before I name it. In the first weeks I'd sit with the Heads of Design and their engineering counterparts and log what makes engineering wait, and why. Then I'd pick the one that costs the most time and can be fixed in weeks."*
   - Your guess, said as a guess: work that comes back for the basics, like missing states or tokens that don't map. Encoding those so work arrives ready to build is fast and visible.
2. **"How would you work with engineering on this?"**
   - *"Design owns the intent: the rules, the heuristics, what good looks like at bol. Engineering owns the infrastructure: the token registry, the data feeds, the checks in the pipeline. Designers make things in a sandbox on bol's real components, and engineering owns production."*
   - New tools get tried first on a shared test bed with the other craft leads, so changes arrive as a joint position, not a design-only ask.
   - Proof: *"At lign.ai, once the engineer had the components in code with tokens, our conversations moved from visual details to user flows. At Culligan I worked hands-on with hardware, software, cloud and DevOps engineers on the IoT stack."*
3. **"Your biggest team was fifteen. bol is much bigger."**
   - *"True, and this role has no reports at all, so headcount was never going to be what makes it work. At Culligan my team was fifteen, but the design language reached the product teams of eight business units. What I'd watch here is how many squads use what we build without asking me."*
   - If he presses: *"I haven't led a design organisation this size. The Heads of Design have, so I'd work through them, not around them."*
4. **"How do you stop this becoming another review layer?"**
   - *"I'd hold myself to waiting time going down, not up. If squads are waiting for me, it isn't working."*
   - Proof: *"At Culligan, building IoT for each market created dependency. The shared sandbox is what scaled, and it's still growing."*
5. **"Culligan ships hardware. We release all the time. What breaks?"**
   - *"The rhythm. Reviews every two weeks were fine when a product took years. Here the standard has to live in the tools, where the work happens. What transfers is how to decide what's shared and what's local. At Culligan every market said it was different, and most of it turned out to be integration details."*
6. **"How would you know it's working?"**
   - In order: less waiting and review time, more work accepted the first time, less rework. Baseline them on the first burden, then track the same three as it spreads.
   - *"The bol numbers are hypotheses until we have the baseline."*
7. **"How did you get eight business units to adopt the design language?"**
   - Be honest that it became mandatory for new products: *"I won't have that lever at bol, so I'd rely on what made it stick before the mandate: visible quality, assets that make the right thing easy, and help when people get stuck."*

### Don't
- **Blame the workflow, not the designers.** Say "design's workflow hasn't caught up", never "designers are slow". Heads of Design will hear their teams in it.
- **No countries.** Ronald means teams and speed. "90+ countries" belongs only in the Culligan qualifier.
- **No review step.** "Review board", "approval" and "sign-off" sound like the queue you just said you'd remove.
- **Don't say "framework".** The JD's anti-archetype is the "framework curator".
- **Don't guess** how many designers or squads bol has.
- **E-commerce gap:** bring it up only if he does, and only once: *"I haven't led product design inside an e-commerce platform. I have made design consistent across eight business units that didn't report to me. Same structural problem, on a slower clock."*

## 3. Other questions he may ask

**"Why this role? You ran a team of fifteen; this one has no reports."**
- *"Because here the design organisation would be my end user."*
- It's craft leadership at the level of the Heads of Craft. The product is how bol's designers work, and how they work with the other crafts.
- Proof: you've built a practice before (agency, 0 to 6 people). At Culligan, design became the standard across eight business units without line authority.

**"What's the business case? How will I know in a year that this role was worth it?"**
- *"Less waiting and less rework. I'd prove it on the first burden, in one squad, before asking for more."*
- Signals, in order: waiting and review time, then work accepted the first time, then more bets tested and wrong ones stopped earlier.
- Cost belongs in it too: a token budget per squad, and compute reported next to time saved.
- Proof: *"At Culligan I owned an innovation budget of around €550K a year and wrote the capital requests myself. When the business units didn't believe in IoT, we self-funded pilots until they put it in their own budgets. Today 80% of commercial products run on it."*

**"How would you work with the other Heads of Craft and the domain leaders?"**
- *"Craft direction is mine. People, delivery and priorities stay with them."*
- The workflow is shared, so you build it together. Set up a shared test bed with the craft leads for engineering, product and analytics, and try new tools there before anyone is asked to use them.
- Co-own competency expectations with the Heads of Design, because they own performance.
- Proof: at Culligan the brand team were co-champions and the category teams kept their own roadmaps. Attendance at the reviews grew because people saw the work getting better.

**"Where should Shophulp, or AI in shopping, go? What's design's role?"**
- *"In an assistant, the interface is the conversation and the product data. Design decides what it may do on its own, when it asks, how it reports back, and how you undo it."*
- Where mistakes are reversible, fix them fast after launch. Where trust is at stake (checkout, payments, returns), prevent them upfront.
- Product data is part of the experience: a seller's messy listing becomes the shopper's wrong answer. `[Hypothesis for bol]`
- Proof: *"At YouTube we found people trusted AI for low-stakes questions and wanted a human for sensitive or urgent ones. I'd expect a similar split in shopping, but I'd test it."*

**"Tell me about a disagreement with engineering or product leadership."**
- IoT fragmentation. Each European market wanted its own build: different backends, servicing, tooling and SLAs. You mapped the differences against the commonalities and held 1:1s with each business. Most "local needs" turned out to be integration tweaks, and you said no to the rest. The shared sandbox is still running and growing.
- Lesson: *"Being firm about the boundary was harder than the technical build."*
- Backup: lign.ai. You disagreed with the AI engineer about onboarding as a business objective. You dogfooded the MVP and shared insight clips instead of a report.

**"Your first 90 days?"**
- **Weeks 1–4:** inside squads in both domains, with the Heads of Design and their product and engineering counterparts. Log what makes engineering wait for design, and why. Pick the biggest burden that can be fixed fast.
- **Weeks 5–8:** baseline it, then fix it with the other crafts in one squad.
- **Weeks 9–12:** share the results and pick the next one. Alongside this, start the shared test bed with the other craft leads.
- Close: *"Nothing I propose before week four should be taken too seriously. bol knows how it ships; I don't yet."*

**"What would you stop doing?"**
- Heads of Design reviewing AI output for the basics. Encode the basics and give their review time back to judgement.
- Proof: you paused the smart bottle to put the team behind Pelican, and kept the prototype and vision video so the idea wasn't lost.
- *"Kill on evidence, not because time or budget ran out."*

**"What do you need from me?"**
- *"Air cover for the why, not a mandate for the how."*
- A squad to start in. Access to tools, repositories and data. Time with the other craft leads, so it's a joint effort from day one.
- Proof: at Culligan the COO was the early sponsor, while adoption was still earned unit by unit.

**"Biggest failure?"**
- The Italy payment pilot (qa-master script). The site was too small, the users weren't tech-savvy enough to give a clean signal, and you let marketing run the execution.
- What you changed: site selection criteria, success metrics agreed upfront, and the innovation team keeping control of execution. Keep it under 90 seconds.

**"Why should we choose you over the other finalist?"**
- Use bol's own words: design leadership, innovation and hands-on AI, in one person.
- *"I've done the hardest part of this role once already: getting design adopted across teams that don't report to me. And I build with the tools myself, so I know where they break."* If it fits, name what you've built: the Unspoken Diary, SunSpot.

**"Are you talking to other companies?"** Only if he asks. Thijmen already knows.
> "I do have another offer, and Thijmen knows about it. It's a good one, so I'm taking the decision seriously. But this role really excites me, and I want to see where this goes."
- If he asks directly whether you've accepted it: *"I have. I wanted to see this process through because this role excites me."*
- Comp, if it comes up: *"I shared my expectations with Thijmen, and they haven't changed."* Don't share Rabobank's numbers.
- Start date: *"I'd agree that with Thijmen."*

## 01 · The end of average (1:00)

AI is making average design free. So my plan is to help bol's designers make what's specific, and spend their time discovering what comes next.

The title is a wink at Todd Rose's book, *The End of Average*. In the 1950s the US Air Force measured more than 4,000 pilots on ten body dimensions, to design a cockpit for the average pilot. Not one of them was average on all ten. The fix wasn't a better average. It was adjustable seats and controls, built for the individual.

That's this deck. AI is excellent at the average. bol wins on the specific: its shoppers, its partners, Dutch retail reality.

The path in one breath. Designers make bol specific by working as makers and spending most of their time on discovery. That only happens if behaviour changes, so I'd introduce two standards: four behaviours, and an AI-team that guards bol's standards. I'd prove it in one squad, sceptics included, and start by taking the basics out of design reviews.

## 02 · AI makes average free, so bol's designers must make it specific (1:30)

The value of design at bol moves to what AI can't produce. Designers get there by working as makers who show, discover and unite.

AI makes the average free for everyone, including every competitor. So design's job is to make bol specific. By specific I mean bol's own context: Dutch retail reality, partner edge cases, what shoppers actually need. Not the generic answer every competitor's AI gives.

A maker does this through three verbs. Shows: turns an idea into something working, on real data, in days, so bol decides on evidence and reworks less. Discovers: uses the time AI frees to decide what's worth building, and to shape agents people trust. That's what designers want anyway, to use their curiosity to build what's next for people. Unites: works alongside product, engineering and data. The artefact is how the crafts think together, so fewer handoffs, and decisions in days instead of sprints.

Why now? The shopper agent is live, and the partner agent is learning from traces and evals. This role prepares the designers who will shape those experiences.

The ask: invest in guardrails and skills now, so the hours AI frees go into discovery and not into more output.

## 03 · Three shifts move designers to discovery, but production has to be fixed first (2:00)

Time for discovery doesn't come free. Production has to be fixed first, with an AI-team, and the other two shifts grow alongside it.

Look at the week. Today most of a designer's time goes into production. By 2028 most of it goes into discovery, and that's a hypothesis I'd measure. Matthew mentioned a KR of six hours back per person per week. Hours freed measure speed. They don't tell you whether the work got better, and if the time isn't redirected on purpose, delivery absorbs it.

AI only gives time back if we invest first: a design language, guardrails, sharper intent, iterated until the rejection rate is acceptable. Without that, the time goes into reviewing output. A product designer on an AI-first project told me she felt like she was babysitting the AI instead of designing. With this structure from the start, she said, she wouldn't be so mentally tired.

So, three shifts, and only the first is a step.

Screens to Intent comes first. It fixes production and frees the time the others need. That's the AI-team, which I'll show on the next slides: commands designers steer, and a crew that checks the basics.

Handoffs to Dialogue. Discovery made in code. A working thing on real data moves a conversation further than a deck. A sandbox lets designers prototype on the real pipeline without breaking anything, with access agreed with Engineering and Analytics.

Domains to Journeys. Designers own journeys together with product, and this grows as trust between domains grows. It could eventually change the design team's operating model, but structures only change after the pilot.

One example on context. At SunSpot the interface looked polished, but the shade was calculated from building centre points. Real data, the city's terrace permit polygons, fixed it. Context is infrastructure.

## 04 · Two standards make it real (0:45)

The standard isn't a document. It's how designers work and the AI-team they work with, written together with the people who'll live by it.

Standards cover two things that need each other: how people behave, and the tools they work with. Behaviours without tools stay a poster. Tools without behaviours generate inconsistency faster.

Tools will change every quarter. The behaviours decide whether any tool produces good work, and the AI-team makes sure the standards are met in practice.

Both are universal at the core and local at the edge. The behaviours and the core crew are the same everywhere. Each domain brings its own evidence and data, and adds its own crew members. Research and data decide what moves into the core.

And it's co-written with designers, researchers and writers. The people we design with matter as much as the people we design for.

This isn't a description of today. It's the target of the change programme, specific enough to recognise, coach and measure. Slide 14 shows where each one shows up in the signals.

## 06 · "Quality is the hard bit": the AI-team checks the basics, designers own the intent (2:00)

The AI-team takes brand, context and accessibility checks off people's desks, so designers spend their judgement on intent. And it's governed together with engineering.

Matthew called defining quality UX at bol "the hard bit", so this is where I'm most concrete.

The goal is an AI-team that holds delivery to bol's quality standards, designed with the designers who use it. It's theirs, not a tool handed to them. It builds trust between crafts, because engineering stops rejecting work for missing states and unmapped tokens. And it gives designers time back.

Generic AI output fails in three ways at bol. Brand drift: styles that ignore bol's colour and component system. Context blindness: short English placeholders instead of Dutch retail reality, like "Morgen in huis", and partner edge cases. Review fatigue: design leadership spends its week reviewing basics. Each crew member answers one of these failures. Each has a role and persona, the tools it may use, which is read access to tokens, staging data and a headless browser and nothing more, and the instructions that govern it. That's the spec card on the next slide.

The crew flags and proposes fixes. It never silently changes the design. The designer keeps the judgement, which is what high taste and high agency ask for.

Designers also get commands: critique, a UX review against bol's heuristics. Adapt, for responsive behaviour. Stress-test, which runs worst-case real data before review. Anyone can propose a new one.

Governance is shared, with one contribution workflow. AI output arrives as a code change, and design and engineering both review it before it merges. Design owns the intent: agent profiles, command logic, heuristics. Engineering owns the infrastructure: token registry, data feeds, CI checks. Every rejection becomes a new rule. With this, the design system accelerates AI. Without it, AI generates inconsistency faster.

I built the /bol commands the same way I built this deck: audit, polish, distill. I go first. And generic output is a missing-input problem, not a prompting problem. The crew gives the tools the input.

## 07 · Designers co-create the AI-team, so it learns bol's quality standards from the people who know them best (1:45)

The AI-team is built like a product, with designers as its users, in five steps from audit to scale. I'll show it on a partner example.

First, why this is an investment case. Every crew member starts from a failure we measured in the audit, so the first bets go where rework costs most. The bets are small and reversible, with gates: one crew member, one squad, a go or stop at month 4. The return shows up in product terms: fewer rejections by engineering, faster validated decisions, more hypotheses tested. It protects what makes bol distinct, because tokens, tone of voice and seller reality get encoded in the crew. And it scales by contribution, not headcount.

Five steps, the same for every command and crew member. One, audit: senior designers review recent AI work to find the most common drift, localisation failures and accessibility misses. That decides what we build first. Two, spec: designers and I write the spec card, with persona, what it checks and what it may never do. Engineering sets token boundaries and data access. Three, user zero: I stress-test it against worst cases, in a shared test bed. Four, pilot: only after it passes, one squad, sceptics included, uses it on real work. If it fails, back to the spec card. Five, scale: designers propose commands to a shared library, reviewed like code, and domains add their own crew.

Here's the partner example. Generic AI generates a bulk price grid with short, perfect titles. In production a seller uploads a 150-character title in capitals with missing images, and the design breaks. Today a Head of Design catches that in review. Instead we build a partner stress tester: a chaotic seller as persona, staging seller data as tools, and instructions to inject worst-case data and flag what breaks, never fix it silently. The squad runs it with one command, and the designer decides. Then it becomes a shared command for any data-heavy team.

The limit: agents only catch what they're told to look for. So the crew covers the basics only, and decisions that matter still go to real sellers.

I already work the way I'm asking designers to work. I took a product from idea to live in eight hours with these tools, which is why I can be user zero.

It sits after the AI-team on purpose, because it's the same idea at small scale. A design system first, then a spec and a rules file the AI follows, then build, test and run. The commands and the crew are this, made shared.

Quickly through the stages. System: a design system from Unspoken's brand book, so every screen started on brand. Spec: a PRD and a rules file, ten tickets small enough to test alone. Design: a happy flow first, then 22 screens across phone, laptop and projector, every state. Build: I plan, review and test. Run: secrets out of the code.

For this plan it means I've built with these tools myself, so I stress-test the crew before any designer touches it.

## 09 · Design and launch the change like a product designers want to use (1:30)

I have no authority over the Heads of Design, so the change has to be pulled, not pushed. I go first, then the sceptics, then the crafts together.

The vision says where we're going, and the behaviours say what good looks like. This is how people get there without anyone being told to.

I have a mandate from the CPTO MT but no line authority. So it has to be adopted. It can't be announced into place. Influence starts with curiosity. Before proposing anything, I want to understand how designers work today and what they struggle with. There's no one-size-fits-all.

Sceptics come first, for three reasons. They strengthen what we already know works. They uncover blind spots, which matters most here, because the risk with AI work is what nobody thought to check. And they make the recommendations credible: if the sceptics helped shape it, others trust it. Enthusiasts matter too. They bring energy, and the sceptics help decide where it goes.

The hard part, out loud. Designers resist a change that seems to threaten the craft that made them good. Proof from people they trust travels further than any mandate. People adopt it when they see fewer review rounds and less rework in their own squad, and when the commands and rules are ones they wrote.

So the rings: I go first as user zero, then one squad with sceptics in, then domains, with the crafts' test bed at the centre. At Culligan, a design language nobody had to adopt became the standard for every new product. I'll come back to that.

## 10 · Prove the AI-team in one squad, then domains adapt it around a shared core (1:30)

Six months to prove the AI-team in one squad, with gates agreed upfront. Then each domain adapts it around a shared core that stays universal.

One roadmap, two tracks. The people track moves designers from accepting the change to making it theirs. The foundation track makes delivery reliable, so there's time to change at all. Each stage answers the question people are actually asking, and has a signal that says when to move on.

I'd start by relieving one journey with a known pain, chosen with the Heads of Design. I'd observe workflows first and build on the pilot Matthew is already running. The baseline covers the time split, decisions made on working artefacts, and rework.

Two gates, agreed upfront. End of month 4: is the squad deciding more on working artefacts, and did discovery time grow? We continue, change course or stop. Month 6, with these targets: 30% of review decisions on a working prototype, 30% of AI output accepted first pass, discovery time up 10 points, cognitive load stable. If they hold, we decide what scales. If not, back to the audit and spec cards. If the data says the standard is wrong, the standard changes.

Shared core, local fit. Behaviours and core crew stay the same everywhere. Domains add their own crew members and commands. A designer council owns updates to the core, so it stays consistent as domains move.

On boundaries: craft direction is mine to lead. Delivery and performance stay with the domains, so nobody loses territory.

## 11 · Rituals in the teams and a shared test bed keep the change open and moving (1:30)

Two concrete actions keep the change alive from month one. Rituals bring designers along, and a shared test bed brings the other crafts along.

Rituals first. The change has to show up in the designers' week, run by the craft leads. In the craft lab I'd start by sharing my own findings and failures. The change will add cognitive load. But being tired from admin and pixel pushing is different from being tired from making something you see land, and the second kind is rewarding. The rituals are a space to unload, to share frustrations and successes.

The risk is that energy drops once the novelty wears off. So I measure contribution, not attendance: work brought, demos given, rules changed. After two quiet cycles, we shrink or stop the ritual and ask the sceptics why. At Culligan, attendance at design reviews grew because people could see the work getting better.

Now the test bed. Design, Engineering, Product, Product Analytics and UX Research overlap on purpose. Blurring the lines is how we learn each other's expertise. The rule: any change to tools or ways of working goes through the test bed first, with simulated users and real data, before it reaches delivery. Two lanes, open to every craft. Lane one is the AI-team: the latest commands and crew live here first, and I stress-test them as user zero. Lane two is new tools. The craft is evolving fast, so we try new AI design and development tools on real work before anyone adopts them. Useful ones become commands or crew members. The rest we drop at no cost.

Each craft brings something. Engineering: pipeline, tokens, staging data. Product: the bets worth testing. Analytics: real data and what to measure. UX Research: real users, where simulated ones fall short.

And one open question for the crafts, which I'd rather put out than answer. If making is democratised and the lines blur, how are our roles redefined? That's something the craft leads solve together.

## 12 · Creating Culligan's first design language (1:15)

I've done this before. At Culligan I got teams to adopt a new design language without authority, because they could see it working.

Nobody reported to me, and getting it adopted was the harder part. Attendance at the reviews grew because people could see the work getting better. Then we made it usable without us, and teams across eight business units took it into their own roadmaps.

The flow: brand values, purposeful, elevated, expert, brave, thoughtful, became one UX character: simple, trustworthy, expert. Each UX block makes one trait tangible. The indicator bar. The hot-water unlock animation, where motion appears only when it matters. The Cold and Ambient screens, where selecting is dispensing.

The result. Short term, each category team redesigned its flagship on the language. Long term, it became the standard for every new product. The bottle-free cooler, the first built on all six layers, won a Red Dot in 2026. I owned the UX layer, co-designed the touchscreen, indicator and accessibility toolkits with our agency, and approved the work.

What I'd change: approval ended up in one central brand gate. That works at hardware speed. It doesn't work at AI speed, which is why at bol an AI-team guards the standards.

## 13 · Success is designers working differently (0:45)

The first sign it's working is designers working differently. Better products follow a few quarters later, and their evidence feeds back into how designers work.

If the change works, it shows up in how people work before it shows up in the product. Behaviour drives product outcomes, and product evidence feeds back into behaviour.

Product outcomes lag two or three quarters, so I'd report them as they emerge, never in place of the behaviour signals. And every speed signal is paired with a quality or people signal.

For bol that means less rework, faster validated decisions, and products that stay distinct when every competitor has the same AI tools. It also keeps leadership patient: early, honest signals instead of a promise.

## 14 · We'll see it in behaviour and time first, then in quality, then in the product (1:15)

The signals move in order: behaviour and time first, then quality, then product. Each is paired with a quality or people check, and read out openly every quarter.

The brief asks for signals that the craft is evolving. These cover behaviour, time, quality, product and people. All targets are proposals, calibrated with the Heads of Design after the month-1 baseline.

Read them in pairs. AI output accepted first pass goes up as work sent back for basics goes down. That shows the guardrails and skills are paying off. If cognitive load rises and satisfaction doesn't, the fault is in how I designed the change.

One metric is the engine: contributions. If designers write their own commands and crew members, the AI-team is theirs, not mine. I deliberately don't track weekly active users of the commands. Usage isn't value.

The four behaviours show up here. Agency: decisions on working prototypes, and contributions. Taste: less sent back, more accepted first time. Curiosity: prototyping on real data. Adaptability: a shorter path to a tested hypothesis.

Two risks. Money: many companies now cap how many tokens employees can use, because without a strategy and the know-how, AI gets expensive fast. I don't know how bol handles this today, so I won't assume. It's something to look at together. The framework needs a token budget per squad, proven commands shared instead of everyone prompting from scratch, cost per tested hypothesis in the read-out, and training in using large language models well. And compute and energy: reported next to time saved, in line with bol's B Corp commitment.

The read-out goes quarterly to the Heads of Design, the other craft leads and the UX Research Manager, so UX keeps a voice in how roles and teams evolve.

## 15 · With time back, discovery gets shorter and richer, and bol makes better product bets (1:00)

With time back, discovery cycles get shorter and richer. Ideas get killed on evidence, not for lack of time, and bol makes better product bets.

This is where it pays off. Everything before it exists to move one thing: where a designer's week goes. Screens to Intent gives time to think. The basics leave the review, and the hours go into framing the problem and reflecting on what the last test taught us. Today that reflection is the first thing to go when the sprint is full. Handoffs to Dialogue makes cycles shorter: a working artefact on real data gets a decision in days. Domains to Journeys makes them richer: research and analytics from the whole journey, so the hypothesis is about what shoppers and partners experience, not what one domain owns. Shorter and richer together is the point. Fast and thin is just more output.

In most teams, ideas die because the roadmap is full, and weak ideas survive for the same reason: nobody had time to test them. When testing is cheap, the evidence decides, both ways. More bets tested, wrong ones stopped before engineering builds them, right ones built with confidence.

For designers, this is where their human perspective counts most. Many are worried about their craft and their future. This is the part of the work that's theirs.

## 16 · Designers stay relevant by owning what AI can't average (1:15)

The role will change either way. The opportunity is for designers to shape it, and my first move takes the basics out of design reviews.

The title circles back to the opening. Designers stay relevant by owning what AI can't average: the intent, the conversation, the whole journey. Many designers are struggling right now, with their craft and with the threat to their future work. That's why they have to be part of the change, including the decisions on how things will work. It's done with them, not to them.

My first move rests on an assumption: reviews of AI-assisted work are eating the Heads of Design's week. Matthew, is that the right hurdle? If it's a different one, the method stays the same. Weeks 1 to 4: sit in on reviews, log what gets sent back and why. Weeks 5 to 6: turn the top three basics into the first crew member's rules, with the Heads of Design. Weeks 7 to 10: run it in a partner squad. Week 11: read out review time per item, and how much now goes to intent.

And why I want this. It's an innovation brief wearing a design hat. I've built practices from zero before, and at Culligan I did it inside a company where nobody reported to me. This time the practice is for designers themselves.

## Back pocket: stories ready if a question opens the door

**YouTube metrics drove workarounds** (pairs with slide 14: why every speed signal needs a quality check)
Agents at YouTube's outsourced support operated with fragmented tooling and outdated information, and their performance metrics were inadvertently driving workarounds and call deflection. The number looked fine while the work got worse. That's why I pair every speed signal with a quality or people signal, and why I don't track usage of the commands. Backstop numbers if pressed: 170K+ cases now run through the new Salesforce Service Console, agent productivity up 65%, CSAT 4.5.

**IoT sandbox at Culligan** (pairs with slides 3, 4, 10: sandbox, shared core with local fit, pull not push)
Business units were sceptical and there was no mandate or budget, so we self-funded a few pilots to prove the case. Once 2 or 3 showed value, the BUs committed budget and put IoT on their own roadmaps. Then markets, mainly in Europe, pushed for bespoke builds. I mapped differences against commonalities, held 1:1s with each business, and found most needs were an API tweak, so components stayed universal. We set a flexible sandbox on shared global infrastructure, and the BUs own their integration. Today 80% of Culligan's commercial drinking water products are built on it, and more businesses are still plugging in. The lesson: offering to build it for them created dependency, and the firm handover was the harder skill.

**Service Design Coach, 2024** (pairs with slide 9: no one-size-fits-all)
Theory needed heavy adaptation to each person's business context, such as coaching someone in a marketplace. What worked: a light common foundation, some reading, then hands-on conversation about their real day-to-day problems, with transferable examples and red flags. I'd go further in that direction next time.

**Alex at Culligan** (pairs with slide 5, only if asked for a coaching example)
He came from a hierarchical team, had nine years of field knowledge of the equipment, and struggled to speak up. I created explicit moments where his opinion was the deciding one, until challenging senior stakeholders became normal. He now runs the full flavoured-water category.

**Smart bottle, paused** (pairs with slide 15, framed carefully)
It was paused for resourcing, not killed on evidence. The prototype and vision video stayed as a reference for the dispenser roadmap. Use it to show how you keep ahead-of-their-time work alive, not as an evidence-based kill.

**Tikkie** (pairs with slide 15)
10 experiments in 3 months, 4 revenue models validated, cashback shipped to production, and a board decision backed by evidence.

**lign.ai workflow** (pairs with slide 3, Handoffs to Dialogue)
I wireframed and spent most of my time on the agents' instructions, a brand designer applied branding to the components, and the engineer put them in a code library with design tokens (I didn't build the token pipeline). I then tested on a high-fidelity coded prototype, and conversations with engineering were about user flows, not visual details.

**Culligan stage gates** (pairs with slide 10)
Three stages: an 8-week time-boxed POC with no BU involvement and a leadership gate, then a shared gate on fit inside a BU, then handover with the BU owning decisions.

**PCB redesign** (pairs with slides 6 and 11: credibility with engineering)

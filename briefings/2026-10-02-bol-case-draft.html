031 · Vision

Screens → intent Done before

The design system has been readable by machines for years. What agents miss is the language: the why, the when, the feel. Writing it down for agents becomes core craft work.

Culligan design language, written for people

Handoffs → dialogue Done before

Artefacts exist to move the conversation forward. A working thing on real data, made in a day, changes the quality of the decision.

Report → design sprint (WEAREREASONABLEPEOPLE); coded prototype testing (lign.ai)

Experience ownership follows the customer across domains, together with product. Domains stay accountable for their systems.

The honest cost: cognitive load goes up, and people protect what made them good. That's why designers are the users of this change.

Why this is more than efficiency

Every craft's tools are now open to everyone. Anyone with an idea can show it as a working experience on real data, as a hypothesis to test. Cycles shorten everywhere. The choice is what to do with the time: ship more of the same, or discover and challenge more.

Culligan design language, layer 4: UX + UI Done before

My part: the personas, journeys and context-based UX character; co-designed the touchscreen and indicator toolkits and the accessibility work; approved the experiences.

Motion that tells the truth about urgency

At bol Adapted

One character, adapted for consumer, partner and core.

When is a standard universal?

Only when a customer crosses the boundary, when shared tools or agents need one source of truth, or when the risk is organisation-wide: accessibility, trust, regulation. Everything else stays with the domain.

At Culligan, most "local needs" in the IoT rollout turned out to be integration differences, not reasons for different components. Done before

The UX layer of Culligan's design language was the part I owned. We started from who actually uses the products, and from that came one UX character, adjusted by context. Then the craft: azure means the same on plastic and on screen; indicators only pulse when it's urgent; and the rules came from real use.

082 · Standards

One principle, written three ways

"Never leave users wondering what to do next."

1 · Why, for people Done before

Users trust the product to be vigilant on their behalf. A dead end breaks that trust.

2 · Rule, for agents Hypothesis

Every agent turn ends in one of three: a clear next action, confirmation that the task is done, or a handover to a person. If it can't finish, it says why and what you can do.

3 · Check Hypothesis

Dead-end rate across traces, plus a human check that the next step is the right one.

PartnerMy new offer isn't showing on the product page.

AgentOffers can be hidden for several reasons, for example stock levels, price or listing quality.

Check: dead end. No next action, no handover.

AgentYour offer is hidden because its stock is set to 0. Update the stock and it will show again. Shall I open the offer for you?

Check: passes. One clear next action, offered by the agent.

Illustrative and scripted. Not bol's partner agent.

The principle comes from Culligan's layer 4. The rule and the check are what I'd write for bol. The same check fits how Matthew's team already learns from traces and evals.

For delegation: "…or what the agent did on their behalf."

103 · Driving change

Tools change in weeks. Habits change in quarters

The change I'd land in six months: write bol's design language for agents on one cross-domain journey, inside the existing pilot, not next to it. Hypothesis

Squad: designer · PM · engineer · one sceptical designer. Journey chosen by data.

M0–1Listen and baseline. Pick the journey. I prototype part of it myself.
M1–3Co-write the first principles, three ways. AI as auditor: edge cases, simulated partner behaviour, labelled apart from real research.
M3–5Run it. Does output pass review more often? Did discovery time grow?
M5–6Read-out to Heads of Design and the MT. Decide what becomes universal. Pick the next two journeys.
Exit criteria, agreed upfront

What the data has to show to continue, to change course, or to stop. If the data says the bar is wrong, the bar changes: it's the product.

One journey, one squad, inside the pilot Matthew is already running. Small enough to land, visible enough to learn from.

113 · Driving change

Designed for people, not announced to them

Test it first, together Done before

Every new tool or workflow goes through a shared test bench with the Engineering, Data and Product craft leads before designers are asked to use it.

Sceptics first

Start with the designers who defend the craft. Their objections show where it breaks.

Relief first

Take one unglamorous task off the Heads of Design before asking for anything.

Clear boundaries

Craft direction is mine to lead. Delivery and performance stay with the domains.

Learn on live work Done before

A light shared foundation, then pairing on real journey work, inside delivery time rather than competing with it.

What gets rewarded Hypothesis

Update competency expectations together with the Heads of Design.

Rituals, built on bol's own
  • Open craft lab, monthly: what we found, what broke, what worries us. I go first, including my own failures.
  • Three-minute demos: show a working thing on real data, not slides.
  • Designer council: rotating and cross-domain, sceptics included; co-owns the language, modelled on bol's culture trust.
Two kinds of reward

Intrinsic: seeing decisions land, backed by data, with less admin. Structural: what the organisation recognises. The first makes people want it; the second makes it last.

We'll be tired either way. Tired from admin and tired from making something you can see land are not the same tiredness.

Proof that reward creates pull: at Culligan, review attendance grew because people saw the work getting better. At WEAREREASONABLEPEOPLE, developers started asking for the sprint format.

What I'd change

Approval ended up in one central brand gate. At hardware speed that works. At AI speed it becomes the bottleneck: encode the checkable parts, keep human review for intent and character.

SignalWhat I'd look at

Quality as a loop, decided by reversibility

Fix fast where an experience is reversible and low-risk. Prevent upfront where the stakes are high: checkout, payments, partner payouts, trust.

What each peer gets
  • Engineering: the language in a form their agents can use
  • Product: evidence, sooner
  • Product Analytics: data and insights open to everyone
  • UX Research: research focused on the blind spots, and organisational memory agents can retrieve

UX joins the agentic development vision as the owner of intent.

Every speed signal is paired with a quality or people signal. If cognitive load rises and satisfaction doesn't, the change is badly designed, not the people.

Business case: from craft signal to business signal

Review rejection rate → review time and rework → time to market. Discovery time → decisions backed by evidence → fewer features built that don't move a metric.

Risks and known unknowns
  • Cognitive load and burnout
  • Journey ownership clashing with domain accountability
  • The cost of maintaining a language for agents
  • Compute and energy cost
  • People changing code they don't understand
  • Simulated evidence mistaken for real research
When energy dips
  • Measure contribution, not attendance
  • A stop/continue rule: if a ritual isn't earning its time after two cycles, shrink it or stop it
  • Rotate hosts; show work, not slides
  • Go to the sceptics first and ask why
  • Sponsor cover for the why, never for the how

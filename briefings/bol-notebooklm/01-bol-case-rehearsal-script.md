# The end of average: bol case, slide-by-slide rehearsal script

Presenter: Marta Axpe. Role: Principal Craft Lead UX at bol. Panel: Matthew Harper (Head of Product Design, currently covering this role ad interim) and Maurice Jongerius. Thursday 8 October 2026, 14:00, in person at the bol office. Format: a 20–30 minute walkthrough, then discussion.

How to use this source: it is the presenter's own deck (17 slides) and speaker notes, cleaned up for rehearsal. Go through it in slide order. For each slide there are four parts:
- **On screen**: what the panel sees. Headlines are verbatim.
- **Say it**: the core talk track. Lines in quotation marks are meant to be said word for word.
- **If asked**: backup material. Don't present it unless someone asks.
- **Bridge**: how the slide hands over to the next one.

Suggested timing: about 24 minutes in total. Checkpoints: slide 6 starts by about 7:30, slide 9 by about 13:30, slide 13 by about 20:00.

The deck uses three tags throughout: **Hypothesis** (something I assume, to be tested), **Risk** (a red flag, and how I'd mitigate it) and **Experience example** (something I've done that supports the argument).

## Storyline in one breath

"AI is changing what a designer's day is made of. My vision is that designers make bol specific: they work as makers, on real data and with real users, and spend most of their time on discovery. That only works if people change how they behave, so the standard I'd introduce is a behaviour framework, made practical through one way of working and an AI-team that guards bol's standards, so designers can steer. The AI-team is the focus of the first 6 to 12 months, because it's what frees the time for discovery. I'd prove it in six months by starting with one squad and the people most sceptical of it, measure whether behaviour changes, and start by making the Heads of Design's job easier."

Four sections: 1 Vision (slides 2–3) · 2 Standards & frameworks (slides 4–8) · 3 Driving change (slides 9–12) · 4 Success (slides 13–15) · Close (slide 16).

## Slide 5 · 2 Standards & frameworks: "Each behaviour is observable, backed by access, time and safety" (about 1.5 min, starts at 6:00)

**On screen:** a table with three columns: Behaviour · What it looks like (with "What it's not" in coral) · How we make it possible.
- **High agency, solves it without waiting.** Builds a working prototype on real data to test a hypothesis, instead of asking for resources first. Brings a proposal with the problem. *Not:* taking the credit and forgetting the team that made it possible. *Made possible by:* access to tools, repositories and data; a dedicated prototyping environment; permission to run small experiments autonomously.
- **High taste, knows what good looks like, and why.** Rejects plausible but generic output and can explain why. Writes intent agents can use. Knows what to remove. *Not:* personal preference, or pixel policing. *Made possible by:* an agent-first design language that captures intent; critiques built on real examples; time to study the best work in and outside e-commerce.
- **High curiosity, reaches for the tool, the data or the source before the expert.** Tries the new tool, workflow or data source first, and shares what it found and where it failed. Goes looking for user evidence. *Not:* chasing every new tool. *Made possible by:* protected time to explore; an open craft lab to share findings.
- **High adaptability, changes course when the evidence says so.** Changes their mind in public. Works across crafts when the work needs it. Comfortable moving between many topics. *Not:* no direction, or constant pivoting. *Made possible by:* short cycles with exit criteria agreed upfront; psychological safety to be wrong.

**Say it:**
- Each behaviour has a "looks like", a "what it's not", and what the organisation has to provide.
- "The last column is my job." If we ask for these behaviours, bol owes people three things: **access** (tools, repositories, data, a prototyping environment), **time** (to explore, study great work and critique on real examples) and **safety** (permission to experiment, short cycles with exit criteria, room to be wrong).

**If asked (experience example):** developing Alex at Culligan. I created explicit moments in design reviews where his field knowledge was the deciding voice, until challenging senior stakeholders became normal for him. He now runs a full category.

## Slide 6 · 2 Standards & frameworks: "'Quality is the hard bit', so an AI-team guards bol's standards while designers steer" (about 2.5 min, starts at 7:30)

**On screen:**
- The goal: an AI-team for the designers that holds delivery to bol's quality standards, designed together with the designers who use it, building trust between crafts, and giving them time back.
- **Commands** (the designer steers; written by designers, in plain language):
  - /bol critique: a UX review against bol's heuristics
  - /bol adapt: responsive behaviour for every device
  - /bol stress-test: fills it with worst-case real data, such as long titles and missing images
- **The crew** (sub-agents work in the background). Each crew member is designed from one archetype profile: a role and persona, the tools it may use, and the instructions that govern it.
  - **System Steward**: stops brand drift; a strict guardian of tokens. Tools: read access to the token repository. Governed by: bol's design system rules.
  - **Context & Data Builder**: stops context blindness; a Dutch retail copywriter. Tools: staging product and price data. Governed by: bol's tone of voice and localisation rules.
  - **Finish Reviewer**: stops review fatigue; acts as accessibility lead. Tools: a headless browser for keyboard and contrast. Governed by: bol's accessibility standard.
- **Shared governance:** one contribution workflow: agent output arrives as a code change. Design owns intent (agent profiles, commands, heuristics). Engineering owns infrastructure (tokens, data, CI checks). Domains own crew members and feedback loops. "Without shared review, AI just generates inconsistency faster."

**Say it:**
- Matthew called defining quality UX at bol "the hard bit", so this is where I'd be most concrete.
- Start with the goal, because the tooling only matters if it serves it. "It's theirs, not a tool handed to them." It also builds trust between the crafts (engineering stops rejecting work for missing states and unmapped tokens) and gives designers time back.
- The three failure modes of generic AI output at bol: **brand drift** (arbitrary styles that ignore bol's colour and component system), **context blindness** (short English placeholder strings instead of messy Dutch retail reality like "Morgen in huis" and partner edge cases), and **review fatigue** (engineering rejects work with missing states or unmapped tokens, and design leadership spends its week reviewing basics).
- Each crew member answers one failure. Each is designed from the same archetype profile, so anyone can specify a new one: its role and persona, the tools it may use (read access to tokens, staging data, a headless browser, nothing more), and the instructions that govern it. That profile is also the spec card on the next slide.
- "The crew flags and proposes fixes; it doesn't silently change the design." The designer keeps the judgement, which is what high taste and high agency ask for.
- Universal vs local: the basics (on-system, accessible, realistic) are universal and checked by the crew, so no human review time goes into them. Intent across journeys stays with the design lead. Fit is local: each domain adds its own crew members, like the partner stress tester on the next slide.
- The three commands. Anyone can propose a new command.
- Shared governance: the design system and the AI-team become one shared engine, not designs thrown over a wall. When a designer uses the AI-team to make a new layout, the output is submitted as a code change, and design and engineering both review it before it merges. Every rejection in review becomes a new rule. If a new interaction is needed, design and engineering build the token or component together once, update the crew's instructions, and from then on the AI handles the execution.
- "With this governance the design system is an acceleration layer for AI; without it, AI just generates inconsistency faster."
- "The /bol commands follow the same pattern I used on this deck with Impeccable: audit, polish, distill." That's proof of "I go first."
- "Generic output is a missing input problem, not a prompting problem." The crew gives the tools the input. The standards grow with use: every rejection becomes a rule or a test example, so the next draft starts closer.

**If asked (how it works):** commands are governed by rule files in plain markdown (for example a critique or polish reference). Crew members are agent definitions with a persona, constraints and tool access. The System Steward reads the token repository; the Finish Reviewer runs keyboard and contrast checks in a headless browser; the Context & Data Builder reads staging data.

## Slide 7 · 2 Standards & frameworks: "Designers build the AI-team: they spec it, pilot it and extend it" (about 2 min, starts at 10:00)

**On screen:**
- Subtitle: built like a product, so bol's leadership sees a return at every step: designers co-author it, and each crew member is a small, measurable bet that cuts rework and frees time for discovery.
- Five steps:
  1. **Audit: find where AI output fails.** Senior designers review recent AI work: where it drifts, misses context or fails accessibility.
  2. **Spec: write the spec card.** Designers and the craft lead co-create each spec card; engineering sets what it can read.
  3. **User zero: I break it first.** I stress-test it in the shared test bed against worst cases before any designer relies on it.
  4. **Pilot: one squad uses it.** Once it passes user zero, a squad, sceptics included, uses it on real work and data.
  5. **Scale: designers contribute.** Designers propose new commands to a shared library; domains add their own crew.
- Example in practice (tagged Hypothesis): a partner stress tester for the bulk price grid.
  - Audit, the gap: AI draws short, perfect titles; sellers upload 150 characters in capitals.
  - Spec, the archetype: co-design a sub-agent that acts like a chaotic seller.
  - User zero, the test: I run it on grids built to break; it must flag, not invent.
  - Pilot, in use: a partner squad stress-tests every grid with one command.
  - Scale, shared: it becomes a command any data-heavy team can use.
  - The result: the grid reaches the engineering sync working, and the design team reviews the behaviour.

**Say it (why this approach, for bol's product and business leadership):**
1. It's an investment case, not a tooling wish. Every crew member starts from a measured failure in the audit, so the first bets go where rework costs most.
2. Small, reversible bets with gates: one crew member, one squad, a go or stop decision at M4, so leadership never funds a big-bang programme.
3. The return shows up in product terms: fewer rejections by engineering, faster time to a validated decision, more hypotheses tested per squad. The hours freed (the six-hours-back KR) are steered into discovery instead of being absorbed back into delivery.
4. It protects what makes bol distinct when every competitor has the same AI tools: bol's tokens, tone of voice and seller reality are encoded in the crew.
5. It scales by contribution, not headcount: designers and domains add crew members, so cost doesn't grow with every team.
6. It's honest about cost: compute is reported next to time saved, in line with bol's B Corp commitment.

And it still lands with the people: designers co-author it, the Heads of Design get the basics off their desk first, sceptics shape the rules in the pilot, and "I carry the risk as user zero." It's built like a product: designers are its users; engineering, product and data are its stakeholders. Same five steps for every command and crew member.

- The five steps in detail: Audit is workshops with senior designers reviewing recent AI-generated work to find the most common visual drift, localisation failures and accessibility misses, which tells us which crew member to build first. Spec is a spec card per command or crew member: the persona, what it checks, what it may never do; engineering defines the token boundaries and what data it can read. User zero: before any designer touches it, I stress-test it in the shared test bed. Does it output valid tokens, does it catch errors we planted, does it flag instead of inventing? Pilot: only once it passes user zero; if it fails, it goes back to the spec card. Scale: designers propose their own commands to a shared library, reviewed like code, and domains add their own crew.
- The example, and my first move with the Heads of Design: in the partner domain, generic AI generates a bulk price update grid with perfect, short product titles. In production a merchant uploads a 150-character title in capitals with missing images, and the design breaks. Today the Head of Design catches that in review. We co-create a partner stress tester from the crew archetype: its persona is a chaotic partner seller, its tools are staging seller data, its instructions are to inject worst-case data and flag what breaks, never fix it silently. I test it on grids built to break. The partner squad runs it on every grid with one command; the crew flags what breaks and proposes fixes on bol tokens; the designer decides; the Finish Reviewer checks that the data density hasn't broken accessibility. Then it becomes a shared command for any data-heavy team.
- This example is framed as a hypothesis on the slide.

**If asked:**
- The risk: agents only catch what they were told to look for. So the crew covers the basics only, simulated data is labelled, and decisions that matter still go to real sellers.
- The same process later works for agent behaviour: the partner agent in beta is a candidate after month 12.

## Slide 15 · 4 Success: "Discovery gets its time back, and bol makes better bets" (about 1 min, starts at 22:00)

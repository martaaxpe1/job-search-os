### Q1: "The engineering craft lead argues AI makes a lot of traditional UX redundant and engineers and PMs can take more of it. How would you challenge that, and how would you redefine the crafts' responsibilities together?"
**Type:** Design leadership / cross-craft influence (peer-level, no authority)
**Your answer:** Engineers think they can design, and designers can also code. Two sides: (1) find the common problem with the other crafts, meaning where engineering waits for design and where product hands designers prototypes; don't protect turf. (2) Earn engineering's credibility, learn their vision, then build a UX path with quick wins. Basic UX work doesn't need endless screens; design moves from a service to a platform that defines the rules of intent (brand, accessibility). Designers commit code. Then a third point: move from domains to journey squads that mix crafts. You closed with "sorry, that was a long answer," a summary, and "designers are a bit protective of their craft."

**Law 1, Structure: 4/10.** You announced two sides and delivered three. The third (reorganise into journey squads) is the largest claim in the answer and came last, unflagged. About 620 words, roughly 4 minutes. The summary at the end helped.
**Law 2, Specificity: 3/10.** No example at all, though you had exact matches ready (below). "Rules of intent" was the right idea but stayed abstract.
**Law 3, Skill: 6/10.** The skill tested was holding design's ground with a peer while agreeing a new split. Not being defensive was the right instinct, and Ronald later said he doesn't expect that pushback anyway. But you never said what design keeps that AI doesn't replace. You said it in Q3 ("designers have the power to empathise with users and a creative power"); it belonged here.

**Overall: 5/10**
**What worked:** "Instead of trying to protect that… find the common problem" and "the system has to change from design being a service." Both line up with his team-cohesion concern.
**Value left on the table:**
- *The proof:* your prep line, "At Culligan I worked hands-on with hardware, software, cloud and DevOps engineers on the IoT stack," and the lign.ai detail: once the engineer had the components in code with tokens, your conversations moved from visual details to user flows (Story 7).
- *The caveat your own case had:* slide 10 says to change structures only after the pilot shows it works ("a direction I'd test before anyone touches how bol is organised"). **Rewrite (about 200 words, about 80 seconds):**
> I'd agree with half of it. AI does make a lot of traditional UX work redundant: drawing every screen, redlines, the hand-off deck. Engineers and PMs will make more of the interface, and that's fine. **Value left on the table:** Your case already had the postpone answer: one squad until month 6, every domain only after that, structures changed only after the pilot (slides 10 and 11). **Rewrite (about 190 words, about 75 seconds):**

### Q4 (follow-up): "Change starts with excitement, then gets messy and slow, and enthusiasm dips. How do you work through that?"
**Type:** Change leadership
**Your answer:** A safe space for designers to share frustrations, fears and findings (the Open Craft Lab from your case). Eat your own dog food: do the work yourself and show rather than tell. Get the sceptics in early for the blind spots and fears; direct the enthusiasts' energy, which can be disruptive too; balance the two. You said: "I don't know exactly how I would do it, to be honest."

**Law 1, Structure: 5/10.** Three good ideas (safe space, sceptics, enthusiasts), not signposted. About 380 words.
**Law 2, Specificity: 4/10.** You named the Open Craft Lab. No real moment where you handled a dip.
**Law 3, Skill: 7/10.** Psychological safety plus sceptics plus directed enthusiasts is exactly right, and he confirmed it with an internal parallel.

**Overall: 6/10.** On content alone, this was your best answer of the call.
**What worked:** "I also need to eat my own dog food… show them, not tell them." And sceptics *plus* enthusiasts was more complete than R2's sceptics-only version.
**Value left on the table:** You have two real dips. (1) Pelican: the team's energy dropped as the project dragged, and your own lesson was to bring sceptics in earlier (you told Anne this at Rabobank, 18/09). (2) The design language: review attendance grew because people saw the work getting better, before anything was mandatory (Story 14). Also cut "I don't know exactly how I would do it." Your case had the format (monthly lab, you go first with your failures), so you did know.

**Rewrite (about 150 words, about 60 seconds):**
> I plan for the dip, because it always comes. Three things.
>
> First, a place where the dip is allowed to show: a monthly open craft lab where people share what broke and what worries them, and I go first, with my own failures.
>
> Second, the sceptics go in the first squad, not the last. They find the blind spots early, and when they come round, others follow.
>
> Third, give the enthusiasts a direction: one clear problem, so their energy goes into the pilot instead of ten side projects.
>
> I learned the second one the hard way. On Pelican the team's energy dropped as it dragged on, and what I'd do differently is bring the sceptics in earlier. With the design language it went the other way: attendance at the reviews grew because people saw the work getting better, well before it was ever mandatory.

*Sources: Story 14 (momentum), Rabobank R3 reflection (interview-history, 18/09), case slide 12.*
